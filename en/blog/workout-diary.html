How to Keep a Workout Diary

A useful log is not paperwork for its own sake. It is a consistent record that helps you recall what you did, compare similar sessions, and make decisions with context.

Reading time: 6 minPublished: November 2, 2026

Why log your workouts?

After a few weeks, session details blur together: which load you used, how many reps you completed, or whether you changed the exercise order. A brief note preserves those facts more reliably than memory.

A log can also help distinguish a one-off fluctuation from a recurring pattern. It does not prove that a particular plan “works” or dictate your next session. It is a source of observations, not an automatic verdict.

What to record

Start with details that let you reconstruct the session. For each exercise, record its name and variation when relevant, plus sets, reps, and working load. Use units consistently—kilograms or pounds—and note whether a load is per side or for the full implement.

  • The date or session label, so entries stay in a clear order.
  • The exercise and relevant details such as machine, grip, or movement variation.
  • Each working set separately: the reps completed and the load used.
  • A brief note when it explains an unusual result or a change to the plan.
Not everything needs measuring. Add context only when it helps explain the entry. Too many fields can make a log cumbersome enough that you stop using it.

How to record sets, reps, and load

Choose a format and use it consistently. For example, “3 × 8” usually means three sets of eight reps, but without the load it does not fully describe the work. “3 × 8 @ 40 kg” adds the load; if sets differed, list them separately rather than averaging distinct values.

ExerciseName + variation
Sets3 × 8
Load40 kg

This is only an example of a format, not a target load or recommendation. For bodyweight, band, or variable-resistance exercises, note the variation and any available resistance measure—or simply record a version you can reproduce next time.

Add notes that matter

Briefly note when equipment differed, a set was interrupted, or you used a new variation. You may also track perceived effort or how you felt, but use a scale you understand and do not treat it as a diagnosis.

A useful note answers, “What might affect this comparison?” Vague labels such as “bad” often explain little; a specific note—such as “shorter rest between sets”—is easier to use when reviewing the entry later.

Keep the log simple and consistent

Use consistent exercise names, load units, and set notation. If you change the format, make the change clear to your future self. After a session, check whether you can read the entry without guessing: what you did, how much work you completed, and under what conditions.
